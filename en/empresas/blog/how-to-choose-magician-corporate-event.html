How to choose a magician for a corporate event: what to ask before you book

October 2026 · 5 min read

Almost nobody books magic for their company twice in the same job. You do it once, with the event already half planned, asking for three quotes and comparing YouTube videos that were all filmed in packed theatres. And the mistake that most often ruins a performance is not choosing a weak magician: it is choosing a good one for a format that did not fit that room.

Choose the format first, then the magician

There are three different ways to put magic into a corporate event, and they have almost nothing in common.

Close-up magic. The magician moves among the guests and performs half a metre away, for groups of six or eight at a time. This is what works at drinks receptions, arrivals, trade fairs and dinners while food is being served. It needs no stage, no microphone and nobody has to stop talking. Everyone notices it because it happens in their own hands.

A stage show. Forty or fifty minutes with everyone seated and facing the same way. This is what fits at the end of a dinner, at a convention or at an awards ceremony. It needs someone to turn the music off, the audience to have chairs and a single point to look at.

Mind reading, or magic with the message inside it. The final reveal is not a playing card, it is the name of the new product, the figure of the year or the campaign line. It is used at launches and conventions, and it is the one that needs the most preparation.

Choosing here is 80% of the decision. A stage show at a standing reception will not work however good the magician is, and close-up magic for two hundred seated people falls short.

The most common mistake

Booking a stage show for a room that is not really a room.

It happens a lot in restaurants with pillars, in long narrow function rooms and on terraces. The magician is good, the act is good, but half the audience cannot see: there is a pillar in the way, or they are fifteen metres back, or people are still talking at the far end because nobody turned the music down.

If your room has pillars, if it is long and narrow, or if the bar will be open at the same time, close-up magic will work far better than a stage show. It is not a scaled-down version: it is a different format, and in those rooms it is the right one.

What to ask

Have you worked this format, not just this type of event? There are excellent stage magicians who are lost at a table of eight, and the other way round. They are two different trades.

What do you need from us? Space, sound, light, set-up time. If the answer is "nothing, we bring everything" without any questions about the venue, be slightly wary.

What happens if the event runs late? They all run late. The director's speech overruns, dinner comes out behind schedule. What you need to know is whether the set can be shortened, moved or split in two without falling apart.

Who exactly is coming? If you are booking through an agency, ask who will perform and whether it will be that person. Get it in writing.

Do you have public liability insurance? Many venues and hotels require it by contract. Asking the day before is a problem.

Do you invoice the company with VAT? It sounds obvious and it is the second most common source of trouble, after timings.

What you need to tell them

Half of all bad quotes come from badly explained briefs. This is enough:

How many of you there are. Whether people are seated or standing. What time it would be and what happens immediately before and after. What the venue is like, even if that means sending a photo of the empty room. Whether there are speeches or an awards ceremony. And whether you want something of the company's to appear inside the act.

One photo of the empty room is worth more than three paragraphs describing it.

The real test

Ask them to tell you how they would do it at your specific event.

A video of a full theatre tells you nothing about what will happen at your dinner on the 12th, in a room with forty people and an open bar. The answer to "so what would you do here?" does.

If they come back with questions — how many of you, how the room is set up, what happens before — that is a good sign. If they come back with the same brochure they send everyone, you already know what kind of performance you are going to get.

When to book

It depends on the season. In October, November and December diaries close weeks ahead and the good dates go quickly, especially Thursdays and Fridays in December. The rest of the year there is more room.

If you have a date, even with the rest of the event only half planned, ask about it. Holding a date costs nothing and so does changing it.

Frequently asked questions

Do you need a stage?

Not for close-up magic: the magician moves among the guests and needs neither a platform nor a microphone. A stage show does need a space of about three metres by two, sound, and an audience that can all face the same way.

Does it work if there are only a few of us? What if there are a lot?

Both, by changing the format. With small groups close-up magic is unbeatable because everyone is a metre away. With two hundred seated people, a stage show. What does not work is forcing the wrong format.

What if it is noisy or the bar is open?

Then close-up magic is the answer. It does not compete with the noise because it does not need to: each set happens inside a circle of six or eight people who are listening. A stage show with the bar open at the same time almost never goes well.
