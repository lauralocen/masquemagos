Christmas magic for town councils: making the light switch-on memorable

October 2026 · 5 min read

There is an image repeated in every town in Spain on the first Friday of December: the square full, children on their parents' shoulders, everyone looking up and waiting. It is one of the few moments in the year when a whole town is in the same place at the same time. It is worth something happening.

The lights switch-on

It is the best-attended event of the whole of Christmas. It also looks a lot like a queue: the square fills half an hour early, children start asking how much longer, it is cold, and eventually somebody gives a speech over the PA and some bulbs come on.

The wait is the problem, but it is also the opportunity. A short magic set before the switch-on turns those twenty minutes into part of the event: people stop checking the time and start looking at the middle of the square.

And then there is the button. That is where you can build something the square is not expecting, and the best part is that the mayor and the councillors are not watching from the side: they are inside the routine.

The magician is covered with a cloth for one second and the person who appears underneath is the mayor, button in hand. Everyone was looking straight at it and nobody saw the switch.

Or the button does not exist until it does: the mayor shows his hands empty, closes them, opens them again and there it is.

Or the councillors come up, each with a closed box that has been in the square since before the crowd arrived, and the countdown numbers appear inside them one by one. Five. Four. Three. With the whole town shouting them.

He presses it, the photograph is his and so is the speech. What changes is that the photograph is no longer the same one as every other year.

The routine is designed with you, around who is going up on stage and how you have it set up, and coordinated with whoever handles the electrical installation so that zero lands exactly. It needs a few weeks' notice, but it gets built.

The Christmas market

A market has its own rhythm: people stroll, browse the stalls, stop briefly, move on again. A stage competes with that and loses, because it asks people to stop doing the thing they came to do.

What fits here is a magician walking between the stalls. He appears in the middle of the walkway, does something for the eight people in front of him, a crowd of thirty forms, and when it breaks up he carries on walking. Nobody has had to sit down anywhere.

And it does something the market benefits from a great deal: it moves people around. The magician keeps changing position, and every time he stops a crowd forms in front of a different stall. People end up walking the whole market instead of staying near the entrance, and the stalls at the far end stop being the stalls at the far end.

Several sets across the holidays

Beyond the switch-on, Christmas is a lot of days to fill: the stage in the square, the sports hall, or whatever space you have set aside for family activities during the school holidays.

What works there is several sets spread out. One in the morning and one in the afternoon, or one each day of the holiday week, always at the same time and printed in the programme. Families plan their visit around those times, and the space has moments when it is genuinely full instead of a shapeless trickle.

If you have several spaces or several consecutive days, sessions can be run back to back. It works out considerably cheaper than booking them separately.

The children's bell-chimes on the 31st

More and more councils hold the chimes at midday, with fake grapes and children who want to toast at twelve noon because by midnight they will be asleep.

Putting magic before the grapes solves two things at once. People arrive in good time instead of turning up at five to twelve with the square half empty. And the children reach the countdown already entertained, which is exactly the part where they have to stand still and wait.

The set leads into the moment of the chimes, and from there the party can carry on: entertainers, costumed characters and hosts so it does not end the instant the last grape goes down.

Frequently asked questions

Does the councillor still switch the lights on?

Yes, and he goes up on stage as well. The mayor and the councillors are part of the routine: they can appear from under a cloth, make the button materialise in their own hands, or open the countdown boxes. He presses it and the photograph is his, only this time the square remembers it. It is coordinated with whoever handles the electrical installation, so give us a few weeks' notice.

Can magic be performed in the square in the cold?

Yes. We have spent a lot of Decembers doing it and the repertoire is already built for winter streets. What you do need is light over the performing area, because it is dark by six and Christmas lighting lights nobody.

How many sets can be done in one day?

On the stage in the square or in the family-activities space, three or four half-hour sets spread through the day is usual. At the market, strolling magic is planned in blocks of one or two hours. We adjust it to how your schedule is built.
