Christmas magic for town councils: light switch-on, market and children's park

October 2026 · 5 min read

Christmas is, together with the patron saint festival, the largest programming block of the year for a town council. It is also where the same thing gets repeated most. Here is which magic format fits each moment, and what to consider so it works outdoors and in December.

The Christmas lights switch-on

It is the best-attended event of the whole Christmas period and usually the worst handled. The problem is familiar: people arrive early, crowd into the square and wait twenty or thirty minutes for somebody to give a speech and some bulbs to come on. In the cold.

Magic there does not go after the switch-on, it goes before. A short stage set, fifteen or twenty minutes, turns that wait into part of the event rather than a queue. And if the switch-on itself can be built into the closing routine —the lights coming on as the payoff of an effect rather than because somebody presses a button— the whole event moves up a category.

That can be prepared, but it has to be discussed in advance with whoever handles the electrical installation.

The Christmas market

Here the format is the opposite: no stage at all. Strolling magic, stall to stall, group to group.

The reason is practical. At a market people do not stand still or look at a fixed point: they wander, browse the stalls and stop only briefly. A stage show competes with that and loses. A magician who appears in the middle of the walkway, does something for the eight people in front of him and moves on, fits the rhythm the street already has.

And it has an effect the traders care about: people stop. A small crowd in front of a stall is people who then turn around and look at what is for sale.

The Christmas children's park

Christmas children's parks —the ones set up in the sports hall or a marquee during the school holidays— have a programming problem: many hours to fill over several consecutive days, and bouncy castles do not carry it alone.

Short, repeated sets work here: three or four thirty-minute sets through the day, always at the same times, printed in the programme. Families plan their visit around those times and the hall gets attendance peaks instead of a shapeless trickle.

The children's New Year's Eve party

More and more councils hold the children's bell-chimes at midday on the 31st. It is a short event, high energy, with children who will not sit through anything long. A twenty or twenty-five minute set before the grapes works well, as long as it is fast-paced and participatory rather than contemplative.

Outdoor magic in December: what to check

It is perfectly possible, but December imposes conditions worth being clear about before booking:

  • Cold affects the hands. The close-up magic done at a cocktail party with a drink in hand is not the same as what can be done at five degrees. The repertoire adapts, but it needs to be known beforehand.
  • It is dark by six. If the event is at 18:00 in the square, you need light over the performing area. Ambient Christmas lighting is not enough.
  • Wind decides. Anything light —cards, paper, cloth— stops being viable in wind. In open squares the set should be planned with that already assumed.
  • Outdoor sound. A stage set in a square full of people needs a proper PA, not a portable speaker. The council usually has one set up for the event anyway; it is a matter of confirming.

When it has to be booked

Councils usually close their Christmas programme between October and November, and the lights switch-on is the first to go because it falls on similar dates across every town in the area. If you are looking at December dates, now is the moment to check availability.

January is another matter: the Three Kings parade is booked separately and much earlier, because there is only one day and everybody wants it.

Frequently asked questions

Can magic be performed outdoors in December?

Yes, but the repertoire is not the same as indoors. Cold affects handling, it is dark from six onwards and wind rules out anything light. The format is adapted to those conditions; what matters is knowing beforehand, not on the day.

What do we need to have set up?

For strolling magic around the market, nothing. For a stage set in the square you need a clear area, light over it and a PA system, which the council usually has set up for the event already.

How many sets can be done in one day?

At a Christmas children's park, three or four thirty-minute sets spread through the day is usual. At a market, strolling magic is planned in blocks of one or two hours. We adjust it to how your schedule is built.
