Christmas magic for town councils: making the light switch-on memorable

October 2026 · 5 min read

There is an image repeated in every town in Spain on the first Friday of December: the square full, children on their parents' shoulders, everyone looking up and waiting. It is one of the few moments in the year when a whole town is in the same place at the same time. It is worth something happening.

The lights switch-on

It is the best-attended event of the whole of Christmas. It also looks a lot like a queue: the square fills half an hour early, children start asking how much longer, it is cold, and eventually somebody gives a speech over the PA and some bulbs come on.

The wait is the problem, but it is also the opportunity. A short magic set before the switch-on turns those twenty minutes into part of the event: people stop checking the time and start looking at the middle of the square.

And then there is the part genuinely worth building. The lights do not have to come on because a councillor presses a button. They can come on as the ending of a routine: inside an envelope a child from the crowd has been holding since the beginning, on a countdown the whole square says together, at the exact moment something that looked impossible finishes happening.

That is the difference between an event that gets done and an event that gets remembered. It is prepared with whoever handles the electrical installation, and it needs weeks of notice, but it can be done.

The Christmas market

A market has its own rhythm: people stroll, browse the stalls, stop briefly, move on again. A stage competes with that and loses, because it asks people to stop doing the thing they came to do.

What fits here is a magician walking between the stalls. He appears in the middle of the walkway, does something for the eight people in front of him, a crowd of thirty forms, and when it breaks up he carries on walking. Nobody has had to sit down anywhere.

And something happens that the traders like a great deal: people stop. A small crowd in front of a stall is people who then turn round and look at what is for sale. It is one of the few activities that directly benefits whoever paid to be there.

The Christmas children's park

School holidays are long, parents arrive exhausted, and the sports hall has to hold up for many hours over several consecutive days. Bouncy castles alone do not carry it: the children tire, the parents sit down in a corner, and by mid-afternoon the whole thing is running on empty.

What works is short, repeated sets: three or four half-hour sets through the day, always at the same times, printed in the programme. Families plan their visit around those times, the hall has moments when it is genuinely full, and parents get half an hour in which their children are sitting still and quiet looking somewhere else. Do not underestimate the value of that.

The children's bell-chimes on the 31st

More and more councils hold the chimes at midday, with fake grapes and children who want to toast at twelve noon because by midnight they will be asleep. It is a short, high-energy event.

A twenty-minute set before the grapes fits well, as long as it is fast and participatory. Children standing up, waiting for the chimes, will not sit through anything contemplative.

What we know about performing in the square in December

We have spent a lot of Decembers doing this outdoors, so the repertoire already comes built for the conditions rather than adapted at the last minute.

It is dark by six, so you need light over the performing area: Christmas lighting is beautiful but it lights nobody. Hands get cold, which rules some things out and recommends others. The wind in an open square carries off anything light, so the set is built knowing that. And if there is going to be a big crowd, sound is what decides whether the back half follows along or drifts away: the council usually has the PA set up for the event anyway, and that is enough.

None of this is a problem. They are simply things worth knowing beforehand rather than on the day.

When it has to be booked

Christmas programmes close between October and November, and the lights switch-on fills up first, because it falls on similar dates across every town in the area and there is only one magician per evening.

The Three Kings parade is separate and closes even earlier: there is only one day and everybody wants it.

Frequently asked questions

Can you make the lights come on inside the show?

Yes, and it is what works best at that event. It has to be agreed in advance with whoever handles the electrical installation so the exact moment is coordinated, so it is not something that can be improvised on the day. Give us a few weeks' notice and it is straightforward.

Can magic be performed in the square in the cold?

Yes. We have spent a lot of Decembers doing it and the repertoire is already built for winter streets. What you do need is light over the performing area, because it is dark by six and Christmas lighting lights nobody.

How many sets can be done in one day?

At a Christmas children's park, three or four half-hour sets spread through the day is usual. At the market, strolling magic is planned in blocks of one or two hours. We adjust it to how your schedule is built.
