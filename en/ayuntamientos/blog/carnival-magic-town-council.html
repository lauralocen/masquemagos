Magic at the town's Carnival: the gap nobody programmes

October 2026 · 5 min read

A town's Carnival almost programmes itself. There is a parade, a costume contest, a dance and hot chocolate. What almost never gets programmed are the gaps: above all, the twenty minutes while the jury withdraws to deliberate and two hundred people in costume stand around waiting for someone to say something over the PA. That is where Carnival deflates, and it is the easiest stretch to fix.

While the jury deliberates

It is the most awkward moment of the afternoon. Every group has paraded, the children have had their costumes on for two hours, it is cold, and suddenly nothing is happening. The jury goes off to one side to add up points and the square is left waiting, with no idea for how long.

What happens next is always the same: families with small children leave. And they leave right before the prizes, which is the part you prepared with the most care.

Twenty minutes of magic in that gap changes the whole afternoon. Not because magic beats the contest, but because people stop checking the time. The jury comes out to hand over the prizes to a full square, which is how it should have been all along.

The prize-giving, with the councillor inside the trick

The envelope with the winner's name can be opened in two ways. One is the way it is opened every year. The other is for the councillor for festivities to show the square an empty envelope, seal it in front of everyone, and find the name of the winning group written inside when it is opened.

Or for the envelopes to hang from a string in full view at the centre of the stage from the start, with nobody able to work out how the names got in there.

He is still the one handing over the prize. The photograph is still his. What changes is that the photograph is no longer the same as last year's, and the winner remembers how the prize was given, not just that it was.

The routine is built with you, around whoever is going on stage and how you have organised the ceremony. It needs to be discussed weeks in advance, but it can be done.

Children's Carnival

In many towns Saturday belongs to the adults and Sunday, or Friday afternoon, belongs to the children: costumes at school, a short procession and a snack in the sports hall or the square.

That format easily holds a forty-minute magic show, with the children sitting on the floor at the front and the parents standing behind. And the child dressed as whatever ends up on stage holding something, because a child in a cape is the best assistant a magician can have.

If you are serving hot chocolate, the show goes first: with sticky hands and a cup to hold, nobody sits down to watch anything.

What is needed

For the stage, a space of three by two metres and sound. If the hall or the square already has a system for the band, we use that one. If not, we bring it.

For the street and the procession, nothing is needed: no stage, no microphone, no power.

We coordinate with whoever runs the sound and whoever runs the event so the set starts and ends at the minute that suits you, without clashing with the parade or the prizes.

Frequently asked questions

What if it rains?

It moves to the sports hall, the community centre or wherever your plan B for the rest of Carnival is. Magic is one of the few things on the programme that fits anywhere, so it is rarely what complicates the decision.

How long is a set?

A stage show runs forty to fifty minutes. To cover the jury's deliberation, twenty-minute sets are usual, which is as long as the gap lasts. It is adjusted to what you need.
