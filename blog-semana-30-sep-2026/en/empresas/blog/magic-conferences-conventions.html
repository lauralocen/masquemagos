How to use magic to break the ice at a conference or convention

September 2026 · 4 min read

Networking is why many people go to a conference, and it's also the part that works worst. Here's what close-up magic does during the breaks, and why it changes the mood of the room.

The networking problem: nobody knows how to start

The scene repeats itself at every conference. The talk ends, people head out to the hall, pick up a coffee and stand there looking at their phone or talking to the two colleagues they came with. The programme says "networking", but in practice everyone stays in their own group.

It isn't a lack of interest. Approaching a stranger without an excuse is uncomfortable, and a coffee on its own isn't excuse enough.

What a magician actually does during a break

The magician moves around the hall and approaches small groups. Three or four minutes of close-up magic with cards, coins or whatever people have on them, then on to the next group.

The trick isn't the point: what matters is what happens straight afterwards. People who have just seen something impossible turn to whoever is next to them to talk about it, and there's a conversation already started. The magician works as an excuse for two strangers to speak.

On top of that, groups open up to make room, and whoever was walking past joins in. It's the most natural way we know of breaking up closed huddles.

The three moments where it fits best

1. Registration and the welcome coffee

This is the deadest moment of the day: people trickle in, collect their badge and wait. A magician in that area turns the wait into the first conversation of the day.

2. The breaks between talks

They're short and people use them to switch off. Close-up magic fits because it doesn't force anyone to sit down or pay attention: you watch it standing, coffee in hand, and it lasts as long as the break does.

3. The closing dinner or drinks

By the end of the conference people know each other a little and are more relaxed. Here magic works as entertainment and, if you want, as a finale with a short act for the whole room.

A note on format: at a conference it rarely makes sense to stop the programme for a show. What works is roaming magic, which fits the timings already in your agenda without taking minutes away from any talk.

Magic can also launch something

If you're presenting a product, a figure or a campaign at the conference, the message can live inside the act: appearing at the end of a trick, coming out of a sealed envelope an audience member has been guarding, or being the prediction revealed on stage.

Companies such as Disney, Honda and SEAT have used this in their presentations. We cover it in more detail in our guide to magic for corporate events.

What the magician needs from the venue

  • For close-up magic, nothing: no stage, no sound, no lighting. Just the hall or the coffee area.
  • For a stage act, a microphone and a space where the room can see properly.
  • The real timings of the breaks, so every minute counts.
  • Some idea of who's attending: a medical conference isn't the same as a sales convention.

Frequently asked questions

Does the magic interrupt the conference programme?

No. Close-up magic happens during the breaks and in the coffee area, without stopping any talk or taking up agenda time.

How many attendees can one magician cover?

One magician comfortably reaches around 100 people during a long break. Beyond that, we usually add a second magician so nobody misses out.
