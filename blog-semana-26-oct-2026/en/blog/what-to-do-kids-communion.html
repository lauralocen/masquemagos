What to do with the children at a communion: the three hours of the meal

October 2026 · 5 min read

The ceremony goes well. The photographs go well. What always gets complicated is what comes after: a three or four hour meal, twenty children in their Sunday best and a room with corridors that are begging to be run down. Twenty minutes into the first course there are already two of them under the tablecloth. And you, who have spent months preparing this, end up eating standing and watching the door.

The problem is not the children, it is the clock

A nine-year-old stays at the table for as long as it takes to eat the food they like. That is twenty minutes. The meal lasts ten times longer.

There is no way of stretching that patience, so instead you spread it out: let them get up, but give them somewhere to go. Otherwise what you have is twenty loose children in a restaurant and one mother per table standing up every ten minutes.

That is why what works best is not the showiest thing, it is the thing that lands at the right moment.

The exact moment

Between the main course and dessert. Always.

Earlier is too early: they are still eating and still entertained by the novelty of the table. After dessert is too late: they have tried the cake, they have got up and nothing will get them to sit down again.

That forty-minute gap between the main being cleared and the cake coming out is exactly where a meal falls apart. It is also where the adults start checking their phones. A show there gathers everyone back at once.

Keeping your child the centre of it

This is what we are asked for most and what is thought about least.

The child making their communion has been the centre of attention all day, and at the meal they stop being it abruptly: the adults start talking among themselves and the child is left not quite knowing what to do in the suit.

A show where they come up, something impossible happens in their own hands and the whole room applauds them gives the day back. They sign a card and that card later turns up inside something their grandmother has had in her handbag since the church. They choose a card without anyone seeing it and it ends up the only one in the deck facing the other way.

They appear in everyone else's photographs. And that is what they remember about the communion, not the menu.

Close-up or a show: which one fits

It depends on how the room is set up.

Close-up magic, table by table. The magician works the room while food is served, stops at each table for five minutes and performs half a metre from people's faces. It works very well when there are a lot of adults and the children are spread across their parents' tables. The meal does not have to stop and nothing has to be set up.

A show for everyone. One hour, children sitting at the front on the floor or on chairs, adults behind. It works when there is a function room with space or a garden, and when there are a lot of children all together. It leaves the best memory if the child making their communion goes up on stage.

There are communions with room for both: close-up during the drinks reception, when people are standing and talking, and a show after the main course.

The adults watch too

This is the part that surprises people. It is booked with the children in mind and it ends up being the uncles and the grandparents who are still talking about it.

Close-up magic is really designed for adults: it happens in the hands of whoever is watching, with no stage and no distance to hide anything behind. A child laughs. An adult goes quiet for a second, which is the reaction a magician is after.

What the restaurant needs to provide

For close-up magic, nothing. No space, no sound, no power. The magician goes from table to table.

For the show, you only need a spot with your back covered. If the room has a sound system we use it; if not, we bring one.

It is worth telling the restaurant roughly what time it will be so the cake does not come out in the middle of the show.

Frequently asked questions

What time should the magician perform?

Between the main course and dessert. That is the gap where the children can no longer sit still and the adults start to drift. If it is close-up magic, table by table, it can start earlier, during the drinks reception or while food is being served.

How many children do you need?

No particular number. It works just as well with five or six as with thirty, only the format changes: with few children close-up magic around the tables makes more sense, and with many, a show for everyone.

What if there are toddlers mixed in with older children?

That is normal at a communion, and it is taken into account when the set is put together. Three-year-olds take in what is visual and ten-year-olds take in what is impossible. What does not work is a show aimed only at the little ones: the older children get bored and leave, and the rest follow.
